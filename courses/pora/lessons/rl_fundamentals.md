## Learning without a model

Dynamic programming needs the transition probabilities $P$ and the rewards $R$. In **reinforcement learning** (RL) the agent knows neither. It learns by doing: in state $s$ it tries action $a$, receives reward $r$ and lands in $s'$. Each **transition** $(s, a, r, s')$ is a sample, and from many samples the agent must improve its policy.

It's how you'd learn a new video game without reading the manual. It's also how a robot could learn a skill too complex to model by hand, such as walking on rough ground.

## Exploration versus exploitation

An agent that always picks the action that looks best so far (**exploits**) may never discover that another action is better. One that keeps trying random actions (**explores**) wastes time on bad ones. Every RL method has to balance the two.

The simplest rule is **ε-greedy**: with probability $1 - \varepsilon$ take the best-looking action, otherwise pick one at random. Other strategies are **optimism** (assume untried actions are good until proven otherwise) and adding noise to continuous actions.

## Worked example: getting stuck on a bad choice

Two grasp strategies succeed with probabilities 0.4 (A) and 0.7 (B), but the robot doesn't know that. It tries each once: A succeeds and B happens to fail, so its estimates are $\hat Q(A) = 1$ and $\hat Q(B) = 0$.

A purely greedy robot now always picks A. Its estimate of A settles near 0.4, still above B's 0, so B is never tried again, and the robot is stuck with the worse strategy forever.

With $\varepsilon = 0.1$, the robot picks B on $0.1 \times \tfrac12 = 5\%$ of tries, so B's estimate climbs toward 0.7 and B takes over.

## Kinds of RL methods

- **Model-free** methods learn values or a policy directly from experience. **Model-based** methods learn $P$ (a dynamics model), then plan with it.
- **Value-based** methods learn how good actions are (Q-learning). **Policy-based** methods adjust the policy directly (policy gradients). **Actor-critic** methods do both.
- **On-policy** methods learn only from data generated by the current policy. **Off-policy** methods can learn from any data, including old experience or demonstrations.

## Why RL is hard on real robots

Learning from scratch can take millions of trials, and each real trial costs time, wear, and sometimes broken hardware. Random exploration can be unsafe. So most robot RL trains in simulation and transfers to hardware (**sim-to-real**), starts from demonstrations, or learns a model to cut the number of real trials.

> **Common mistake:** rewarding a proxy instead of the goal. Reward a robot for "gripper touching the cup" and it may learn to tap the cup over and over without ever lifting it. RL optimizes exactly what you reward, so test the reward on the shortcuts it allows.
